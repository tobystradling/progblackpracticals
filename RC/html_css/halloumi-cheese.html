Halloumi

Shopping list

  • 2 Litres of Sheep's or Goat's Milk
  • 2 lemons, juiced
  • Salt

Recipe

  1. Curdle the milk: Heat the milk in a large pot, stirring so it doesn’t scorch. When it froths up, turn off the heat and stir in the lemon juice. Let it stand for about 15 minutes, until curds form and separate from the whey.
  2. Drain: Pour the curds into a cheesecloth-lined sieve. Let them drain for about an hour, then gather the cloth and gently squeeze out excess whey. Save the whey.
  3. Shape and cook: Tie the cloth around the curds. Bring a pot of water to a gentle boil with a little salt, then simmer the wrapped curds for about an hour, turning once halfway through.
  4. Cool and store: Remove the cheese and let it cool. For a traditional touch, fold in mint. Refrigerate it in the reserved whey with a little salt.
  5. Serve: Slice and pan-fry or grill until golden on both sides. Halloumi also works well in salads, sandwiches, and skewers.

History of Halloumi

Halloumi is closely associated with Cyprus, where it became part of everyday rural cooking. Its brined, durable form helped preserve milk, and village communities traditionally made it from sheep’s and goat’s milk. One of the earliest surviving written references in Cyprus dates to around 1554, when it was recorded as “calumi.” The cheese’s history is older and its exact origins are uncertain; claims that it was first made by Bedouins are often presented as a possibility rather than a settled fact.

The name has linguistic roots in Arabic and Coptic words for cheese. In Cyprus, halloumi is often folded with mint, and its distinctive firmness lets it brown on a grill or in a pan without melting away.
